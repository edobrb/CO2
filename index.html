The loops that do the driving

We emit the carbon — but the carbon is not what does most of the warming. It nudges the system, and the system amplifies its own nudge. Think of a microphone held too close to its speaker: the ear-splitting squeal isn't the singer, it's the loop. Climate has a room full of them. Some push (amplifying), some pull back (stabilizing), and the margin between them is the whole story.

The planet's feedback ledger

How hard each loop pushes, per degree of warming it responds to (IPCC AR6). Bars to the right add heat; bars to the left shed it. Whiskers show the assessed uncertainty range — note that clouds could still turn out to cool.

Read the bottom bar carefully. Stabilization wins — but only by about a third of its own strength. Had the amplifiers summed past 3.2, warming would have fed itself without limit and Earth would look like Venus. Instead the books close at −1.16, and that razor-thin margin is precisely what sets climate sensitivity near 3 °C per doubling of CO₂. We are not living on a stable planet. We are living on a barely stable one.

Why the countdown below is generous

Carbon budgets are usually quoted for our emissions alone — as if the planet were a passive container. Add the carbon that thawing permafrost and northern wildfires will release, and the room left under 1.5 °C shrinks by 25% ± 12%; under 2 °C, by 17% ± 7%. The loops don't wait for the budget to be spent. They are already spending it alongside us.

Hiroshima bombs of extra heat trapped by the oceans and air

🦕 Deep-time déjà vu

The last time CO₂ was this high was the Pliocene, ~3–5 million years ago. No humans existed; seas stood 10–20 metres higher; camels lived in the Arctic. We rebuilt that atmosphere in one lifetime.

🫁 Every breath you take

Roughly 1 molecule in 2,300 of the air in your lungs right now is CO₂ — half again as much as anyone alive in 1850 inhaled. Your grandparents literally breathed different air.

🚄 Speed matters

Coming out of the last ice age, CO₂ rose ~80 ppm in 6,000 years. We just did ~80 ppm in 45 years — about 130× faster. Nature has brakes; we cut them.

🧊 A blanket, not a bomb

CO₂ works like a blanket around the planet. Each year we knit it thicker — the extra heat it now traps equals ~10 Hiroshima explosions per second, ~90% of it soaked up by the ocean.
