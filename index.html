The loops that do the driving

We emit the carbon — but the carbon itself does only part of the warming. It gives the climate a push, and the climate then amplifies that push on its own. Think of a microphone held too close to its speaker: the ear-splitting squeal is not the singer's voice — it is the loop. The climate system is full of loops like that. Some push the warming further (amplifying), some pull it back (stabilizing), and the margin between the two sides is the whole story.

The planet's feedback ledger

How strongly each feedback pushes the climate, for every degree of warming it reacts to (IPCC AR6). Bars pointing right add heat; bars pointing left shed it. The whiskers show the assessed uncertainty range — note that clouds could still turn out to cool.

Read the bottom bar carefully. The stabilizing side wins — but only by about a third of its own strength. If the amplifiers had added up to more than 3.2, warming would have fed on itself without limit, and Earth would look like Venus. Instead the ledger closes at −1.16, and that thin margin is exactly what sets climate sensitivity near 3 °C per doubling of CO₂. We are not living on a stable planet. We are living on a barely stable one.

Why the countdown below is generous

Carbon budgets usually count only our emissions, as if the planet itself added nothing. Add the carbon that thawing permafrost and northern wildfires will release, and the room left under 1.5 °C shrinks by 25% ± 12% — under 2 °C, by 17% ± 7%. The feedback loops are not waiting for us to spend the budget. They are already spending it with us.

And the loops holding the line

To be fair, the ledger has another column. Three loops push back against the warming — and one of them is the only reason Earth is livable at all.

Hiroshima bombs' worth of extra heat trapped by the oceans and air

🦕 Deep-time déjà vu

The last time CO₂ was this high was the Pliocene, 3–5 million years ago. No humans existed, the seas stood 10–20 metres higher, and camels lived in the Arctic. We rebuilt that atmosphere within a single lifetime.

🫁 Every breath you take

About 1 molecule in every 2,300 of the air in your lungs right now is CO₂ — roughly 50% more than anyone alive in 1850 inhaled. Your grandparents literally breathed different air.

🚄 Speed matters

Coming out of the last ice age, CO₂ rose by about 80 ppm over 6,000 years. We have just done 80 ppm in 45 years — roughly 130× faster. Nature has brakes; we cut them.

🧊 A blanket, not a bomb

CO₂ works like a blanket around the planet, and every year we knit it a little thicker. The extra heat it now traps equals about 10 Hiroshima explosions per second — and roughly 90% of it is absorbed by the ocean.
